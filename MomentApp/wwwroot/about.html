About

Conversations deserve a closing ritual.

Most tools are built to remember. Moment is built to forget — on a timer everyone in the room can see.

Presence over permanence

Every messaging app you use is an archive with a chat window attached. The conversation is the interface; the record is the product. That trade is invisible until the day it matters — a subpoena, a breach, a screenshot, a colleague scrolling back through eighteen months of context.

Moment inverts it. A room exists for as long as you set it to, and then it is gone: not archived, not soft-deleted, not recoverable by an administrator. The countdown is visible to everyone in the room, because knowing a conversation will end changes how people speak in it.

What we keep, and what we do not

Being specific matters more than sounding reassuring, so here is the whole of it.

  • No accounts. There is no sign-up, no email, no phone number. A participant is a display name and a colour that exist only inside one room.
  • Messages live in memory. They are held in the running process for the life of the room and are never written to a database or a log file. When the room expires the memory is released and there is nothing left to recover.
  • Calls are peer-to-peer. Audio and video travel directly between browsers. The server helps the two ends find each other and then steps out of the path entirely — it never receives the media, so it cannot record it.
  • Nobody owns the room. Anyone can call a vote to end it. A majority closes it. Saving a transcript needs a unanimous vote and writes a file to your own device, never to us.

What we will not claim

We do not block screenshots. No web application can, and a product that promises it is asking you to trust something it cannot deliver. Anyone in a room can photograph their own screen, and no amount of engineering changes that. What we can promise is that we keep nothing — so the only copy that can ever exist is one a participant deliberately chose to make.

We also do not claim end-to-end encryption for text. Messages pass through the server in memory so the room can show them to everyone present. Calls, being peer-to-peer, are encrypted between browsers by WebRTC as a matter of course.
